3D printing: choosing a setup

Start with the job

  1. Ordinary model or prototype? Start with PLA and a 0.4 mm nozzle.
  2. Need flexibility, outdoor durability, heat resistance or wear resistance? Choose a filament for that requirement, then check the printer profile.
  3. Check whether the filament is abrasive; use the specified wear-resistant nozzle and feed components.
  4. Choose nozzle diameter for the smallest feature and desired print time.
  5. Match the actual build plate, adhesion / release instructions and enclosure requirements to the exact filament.

Nozzle size

DiameterUse whenTradeoff
0.2 mmMiniatures, small lettering and fine features.Slow; small opening raises clogging risk.
0.4 mmGeneral parts, prototypes and learning projects.A useful default balance.
0.6 mmLarger functional parts and wider extrusion lines.Less fine detail; potential speed gain depends on hotend flow and settings.
0.8 mmLarge jigs, fixtures and rough prototypes.Coarse detail; needs a matching slicer profile and enough flow.

A larger nozzle does not automatically make a stronger part. Material, layer bonding, walls and print orientation still matter. Follow the filament maker’s minimum nozzle diameter for filled blends.

Nozzle material

NozzleTypical roleCheck before use
BrassStandard, non-abrasive filaments on compatible printers.Abrasive additives cause wear. Not every printer offers brass nozzles.
Stainless steelA printer-specific nozzle option for compatible materials.Not automatically a wear-resistant upgrade; nozzle material alone does not establish food safety.
Hardened steelAbrasive blends such as carbon- or glass-filled filament.Use the required diameter and profile; hardened gears or other upgrades may also be required.

Filament

MaterialChoose forMain limit
PLAModels, learning, decorative parts and easy prototypes.Limited heat resistance; not the first choice for flexible or demanding outdoor parts.
PETGGeneral utility parts needing more toughness than PLA.Can string; adhesion and release depend on the plate.
TPUFlexible feet, grips and bumpers.Not rigid; feed path and hardness affect printer compatibility.
ABSTough functional parts needing more heat resistance than PLA.Warping and emissions; controlled enclosure and suitable ventilation.
ASAOutdoor parts where UV and weather resistance matter.Enclosure / ventilation requirements and warping.
PA / nylonMechanical parts needing toughness and wear resistance.Moisture sensitivity; drying and dimensional control.
PCHigher-temperature engineering parts on a capable printer.Demanding temperature and adhesion requirements; blends differ.

Filled versions can change the requirements substantially. These are material-family starting points, not ratings for a particular spool or finished part.

Enclosure and bed

FilamentEnclosure starting pointBed / adhesion starting point
PLAUsually open or vented; avoid excess chamber heat.Clean compatible plate; use the plate’s actual profile.
PETGUsually optional.Check release-layer instructions, especially on smooth surfaces.
TPUUsually not needed.Follow plate-specific release guidance and feed-path restrictions.
ABS / ASAEnclosed, stable conditions generally preferred.Warm bed with the specified surface and adhesion system.
PA / nylonEnclosure often helpful; dry filament is important.Drying and adhesion vary strongly by formulation.
PCEnclosure generally helpful on a capable printer.High-temperature profile and specified adhesion / release layer.

An enclosure is not a substitute for ventilation. Glue can be a release barrier as well as an adhesion aid. Select the actual plate and nozzle in the slicer; do not copy another material’s temperatures.

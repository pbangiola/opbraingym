Fasteners

Materials down the left; fasteners across the top. A practical overview of 90 fastener types and 21 material groups. Families include common shop and home-maintenance hardware; this is not a catalog of every industrial standard, size, grade or proprietary design.

D = direct grip, with required pilot / clearance holes. B = through-hole with nut or mating post. T = tapped hole. I = installed threaded insert / nut. A = accessory to matching hardware, not direct attachment. C = conditional: exact product, geometry or system must establish use. — = no general route recommended here; not proof no specialized product exists.

The matrix is an editorial selection overview synthesized from the sources below, not a manufacturer-tested compatibility or load-rating chart. The row describes the material carrying the connection. A steel bracket can be attached to wood with a wood screw; that does not mean the screw can thread into steel. Nuts and washers depend on their mating hardware, so their accessory columns are deliberately different from direct-grip columns.

Before selecting a size: match thread diameter and pitch, head / drive, grip length, strength grade, coating, edge distance and the receiving material. Pilot dimensions, engagement and tightening torque come from the chosen product or joint design. Treated wood is also a wood species; exterior exposure does not turn an indoor fastener into an outdoor one.

For structures, lifting, vehicles or electrical installations, use the specified rated fastener and installation system. Similar appearance does not establish interchangeability. Plastic creep, brittle sheet cracking, wood splitting, thin-sheet pull-out and corrosion can govern the connection before the fastener itself fails.

This wide matrix is best read on screen. For printing, select a fastener family or relevant columns in your print workflow; the full chart exceeds ordinary paper width.

Head shapes, drives, threads and fastener materials

Flat / countersunk heads need a matching seat. Pan, round, button and truss heads bear above the surface. Hex and socket heads describe how the tool engages; Phillips, slotted, square, Torx-type and hex-socket drives do not determine substrate compatibility. Inch and metric machine threads must match diameter and pitch exactly.

Steel strength grades, stainless alloys, brass, bronze, aluminum and polymer fasteners have different properties. Match corrosion resistance, mechanical requirements and mating metals; “stainless” is not a universal strength or galvanic-compatibility rating. Ordinary electroplated zinc is not blanket approval for treated lumber or coastal work. Simpson Strong-Tie exposure guidance.

Material notes
  • Softwood: Predrill where required; check edges and end grain.
  • Dense hardwood: Pilot holes and appropriate driving torque reduce splitting.
  • Plywood: Face fastening differs from fastening into a thin edge.
  • OSB: Use the specified sheathing schedule for structural work.
  • MDF / hardboard: Edges can split; ordinary wood threads are not always the best choice.
  • Particleboard / melamine board: Select for the core, not the decorative surface.
  • Pressure-treated / exterior wood: Use fastener and connector corrosion protection approved for the treatment and exposure.
  • Wood–plastic composite decking: Use the decking maker’s fastening system; screws normally anchor in the frame.
  • Thin mild-steel sheet: Thickness and screw drill capacity govern engagement.
  • Thick mild steel / cast iron: Usually drill and tap, or drill through for a nut.
  • Thin stainless-steel sheet: Requires a fastener suited to stainless substrate hardness and thickness.
  • Thick stainless steel: Match threads; control galling using the assembly maker’s procedure.
  • Aluminum sheet: Check thread engagement and galvanic compatibility.
  • Solid aluminum / die castings: Tapped holes or appropriate inserts; avoid stripping.
  • ABS / nylon: Boss geometry and hole size matter; resin grades differ.
  • PE / PP: Soft, creep-prone thermoplastics; use a designed joint.
  • Rigid PVC: Use the part maker’s hole and expansion allowances.
  • Acrylic / PMMA: Prefer clearance holes and controlled clamping; avoid wedging heads.
  • Polycarbonate: Allow expansion and avoid stress concentration.
  • FDM 3D-printed thermoplastic: Layer direction, walls, infill and heat resistance change joint behavior.
  • Thermoset / fiberglass composite: Will not remelt for heat-set inserts; laminate design governs drilling and fastening.
